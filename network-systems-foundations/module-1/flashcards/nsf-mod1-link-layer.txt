What four problems does the link layer exist to solve?	<div style="text-align:left">• <b>Encoding</b> - structure raw bits so each side knows who the data is for and how to interpret it<br><br>• <b>Error handling</b> - detect, and ideally correct, bits flipped in transit<br><br>• <b>Medium sharing</b> - coordinate access when multiple nodes share the medium<br><br>• <b>Extending the LAN</b> - reach beyond the physical limits of a single transmission medium</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1
Where is the link layer implemented, and why does that placement shape its design?	<div style="text-align:left">In <b>hardware on the network interface card</b>, not in the OS or your application.<br><br>Consequences:<br>• Address filtering and checksums cost the CPU nothing<br>• Algorithms must be implementable in simple silicon or firmware<br>• Anything that cannot be computed as bits stream off the wire is off the table</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1
What are the three parts of a frame, and why does the error check sit in the trailer?	<div style="text-align:left"><b>Header, payload, trailer.</b> The payload is the data the sender wants to exchange; header and trailer are metadata, each divided into fields.<br><br>The frame check sequence goes at the <b>end</b> because the receiver can only verify it once every preceding bit has arrived. Putting it last lets the check happen inline as the frame streams in, with no buffering.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Encoding-into-Frames
What job does Ethernet's type field do, and what general principle does it illustrate?	<div style="text-align:left">It tells the receiver <b>how to interpret the payload</b>, so the frame can be handed to the right protocol above. 0x0800 means the payload is an IPv4 packet.<br><br>This is <b>demultiplexing</b>, the mechanism layering actually runs on. Every layer carries a field naming the layer above it, which is how one wire carries many protocols and why you can add a new one without changing the layer beneath.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Encoding-into-Frames
What is a MAC address, and what assumption about them no longer holds?	<div style="text-align:left">A <b>48-bit link-layer identity</b> for a NIC, written in hex. Frames carry a source and destination; the NIC uses the destination to filter out frames not meant for it. All 1s is the <b>broadcast address</b>, meaning every node on the network.<br><br>Addresses are <b>flat</b>, with no structure to aggregate on. That is why they don't scale past a LAN and why IP exists.<br><br>They were once globally unique, assigned through manufacturing. <b>Virtualization broke that</b>: hypervisors hand out whatever addresses they like, so uniqueness is a local convention, not a guarantee.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Encoding-into-Frames
What is the minimum bar for link-layer error handling, and why not aim higher?	<div style="text-align:left">The floor is <b>detection</b>. Correction is nice but not required.<br><br>Correction needs redundancy on every frame, paid whether or not an error occurs. Detection plus retransmission pushes the cost onto the rare failure case instead of the common one.<br><br>The reusable form: <b>detect cheaply, recover expensively</b>, when failures are rare.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Error-Handling
How does parity work, and why is it weak?	<div style="text-align:left">Append one bit so the total number of 1s is even (or odd, by convention). The receiver recounts; wrong parity means an error. Counting is just XOR across all the bits.<br><br><b>Two flipped bits cancel out</b> and the frame passes as clean. Any even number of errors is invisible.<br><br>The lesson: an error check is not pass/fail, it is a <b>class of errors it can catch</b>. Know which class you bought.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Error-Handling
What does CRC buy over parity, and why was it chosen for Ethernet?	<div style="text-align:left">Treat the frame as a number, divide by a standard generator polynomial, send the remainder. The receiver divides the whole thing; a nonzero remainder means an error.<br><br>It catches any single-bit error, any odd number of bit errors, two adjacent errors, and <b>burst errors up to 32 bits</b>, which is what real physical media actually produce.<br><br>The deciding factor was <b>implementability</b>: the division is only XOR, so it computes incrementally as bytes arrive, in cheap hardware, at line rate. Detection power alone did not win it.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Error-Handling
Link-layer error detection is per-hop. What does that mean for software above it?	<div style="text-align:left">The FCS is <b>recomputed at every hop</b>. It protects one wire, not the path.<br><br>So corruption inside a switch, in memory, or at any point between the checks goes undetected by the link layer.<br><br>This is why integrity checks exist <b>again</b> at higher layers (TCP checksums, application hashes). If your data must be right end to end, the guarantee has to come from the ends, not from any single hop.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Error-Handling
Why does a shared medium need an access protocol?	<div style="text-align:left">On a shared cable or a wireless channel, two nodes transmitting at once produce <b>interference</b>, and neither transmission is usable. Not a slowdown, a total loss.<br><br>So the nodes need an agreed way to <b>share the link fairly and efficiently</b>. Those two goals pull against each other, which is what the whole protocol family is arguing about.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
What are the two families of multiple-access protocols, and what is the tradeoff?	<div style="text-align:left"><b>Channel partitioning</b> - slice the link and assign pieces to nodes, by time (TDMA) or frequency (FDMA). Collision-free and perfectly fair, but a node with nothing to send wastes its slice while a busy node waits its turn.<br><br><b>Random access</b> - nodes transmit uncoordinated and handle collisions when they happen. Efficient when the link is quiet, degrades as contention rises.<br><br>This is <b>static allocation versus statistical multiplexing</b>, and it recurs everywhere: thread pools, rate limits, capacity reservations. Bursty traffic favours sharing; predictable traffic favours partitioning.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
What three questions must any random-access protocol answer?	<div style="text-align:left">• <b>When do I send?</b> Immediately, or check first?<br><br>• <b>How do I know it worked?</b> Can I detect failure myself, or do I need the receiver to tell me?<br><br>• <b>What do I do when it fails?</b> Retry now, or wait, and for how long?<br><br>Same three questions define any retry policy you will ever write.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
What is CSMA, and why do collisions still happen under it?	<div style="text-align:left"><b>Carrier Sense Multiple Access</b>: listen to the channel first, transmit only if it sounds idle. Used by both Ethernet and Wi-Fi.<br><br>It does not eliminate collisions because <b>signals take time to propagate</b>. Another node may already be transmitting, with its signal not yet arrived at you. You sensed an idle channel that was not idle.<br><br>The general point: in a distributed system, <b>what you observe is the past</b>. Listen-before-act narrows the race window, it does not close it.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
Why does Ethernet use collision detection and Wi-Fi collision avoidance?	<div style="text-align:left"><b>Ethernet (CSMA/CD)</b> - the NIC can read while transmitting. If what it reads differs from what it sent, that is a collision. It then jams the channel so every node notices.<br><br><b>Wi-Fi (CSMA/CA)</b> - cannot do that. A wireless NIC cannot listen while transmitting, and <b>hidden terminals</b> exist: two nodes both in range of the access point but out of range of each other never hear one another. So Wi-Fi relies on an <b>explicit acknowledgment</b>; no ack in time implies a collision.<br><br>Same problem, different physics, different protocol. The medium's constraints dictate the design.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
Why is retransmission backoff both random and exponential?	<div style="text-align:left"><b>Random</b> because if both colliding nodes wait the same interval, they collide again, forever. Randomness breaks the symmetry; the lower draw wins and the other senses a busy channel and defers.<br><br><b>Exponential</b> because repeated collisions signal heavy contention. Growing the wait each time lets the backoff adapt to load without anyone measuring it.<br><br>This is the same reasoning behind <b>exponential backoff with jitter</b> in API clients and job retries. Fixed-interval retry across many clients is a thundering herd.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Sharing-the-Link
What problem do switches solve compared to the original shared-cable Ethernet?	<div style="text-align:left">Original Ethernet was one cable with every machine clamped onto it, so every node shared one collision domain. Adding nodes or length raised collisions and capped the network.<br><br>A <b>switch</b> gives each node its own <b>point-to-point link</b> and forwards frames only toward the destination.<br><br>The result: contention shrinks to a single link instead of the whole segment, so the network scales with nodes rather than degrading.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Local-Area-Network
How does a switch's MAC address table get populated?	<div style="text-align:left">The table maps destination MAC to output port, and it <b>starts empty</b>.<br><br>• Frame for an unknown destination → <b>flood</b> it out every port. Nodes it was not meant for ignore it<br>• Meanwhile, read the frame's <b>source address</b> and record which port it arrived on<br>• The reply teaches the switch the other direction<br><br>Nobody configures this. The switch learns the topology from <b>traffic it was already forwarding</b>, and correctness never depends on having learned yet, only efficiency.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Local-Area-Network
Why are loops catastrophic at layer 2 in a way they are not at layer 3?	<div style="text-align:left">An Ethernet frame has <b>no hop count or TTL</b>. Nothing in the frame limits how long it can circulate.<br><br>Combine that with flooding: an unknown destination or a broadcast gets copied out every port, each switch floods it onward, and copies multiply around the loop. The network <b>amplifies its own traffic</b> until it collapses.<br><br>IP survives loops because every packet carries a TTL that decrements. Layer 2 has no such fuse, so loops must be prevented rather than absorbed.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Local-Area-Network
What are the approaches to layer-2 loops, and what does each cost?	<div style="text-align:left">• <b>Track frames already seen</b> and drop repeats - costs per-frame state in every switch<br><br>• <b>Limit hops</b> - cheap, state lives in the frame, but caps reach and needs a header field Ethernet lacks<br><br>• <b>Spanning tree</b> - disable links until the topology is loop-free. Automatic, but you pay for links you cannot use<br><br>• <b>Architect it away</b> - design the topology so loops cannot form, or pre-populate MAC tables so flooding never happens. Most control, least flexibility<br><br>Spanning tree dominates general networks; data centers take the last option.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Local-Area-Network
What is a wireless access point the analogue of, and what is its non-obvious behavior?	<div style="text-align:left">It plays the role a <b>switch</b> plays in wired Ethernet, and typically connects into a wired switch network to reach beyond its own radio range.<br><br>The non-obvious part: <b>all traffic goes via the access point</b>. Two laptops sitting next to each other, well within range, still relay every frame through the AP rather than talking directly.<br><br>So AP capacity, not the distance between clients, is what bounds a wireless LAN.</div>	MSCS Link-Layer Module-1 Local-Area-Network

What is the BGP vulnerability RPKI addresses, and what can an attacker do once traffic is redirected?	BGP communicates paths to reach IP prefixes, and an AS can announce a prefix it does not own.<br><br>Once traffic is drawn to the attacker:<br>• <b>Deny service</b> by dropping everything<br>• <b>Impersonate</b> the real destination, serving a fake site to harvest credentials<br>• <b>Man in the middle</b>, forwarding traffic on to the real destination so the client notices nothing while the attacker eavesdrops
In the concrete example, AS3 legitimately owns 1.1.1.0/24. Why does AS1 send traffic to the attacker in AS4 instead?	AS1's RIB holds two routes for the prefix:<br><br>• <b>1.1.1.0/24 path AS4</b>, next hop 4.4.4.1<br>• <b>1.1.1.0/24 path AS2 AS3</b>, next hop 2.2.2.1<br><br>BGP prefers the shorter AS path, so the attacker's single-hop announcement wins.
What is the idealized solution to prefix hijacking?	• The prefix owner tells every AS which AS it lives in ("I own 1.1.1.0/24 and I'm in AS3")<br><br>• BGP still chooses the path freely, but the path must <b>originate</b> in that AS<br><br>• AS1 then discards the AS4 route because it does not end in AS3, leaving only the legitimate one
What does RPKI stand for, and who acts as the Certificate Authorities?	<b>Resource Public Key Infrastructure.</b><br><br>The Regional Internet Registries (RIRs) are the CAs. They already allocate IP address space, so:<br>• There is an existing business relationship to verify identity<br>• They are a small, well known set that can be trusted
What does an RPKI digital certificate bind together?	• The IP prefix<br>• The prefix length and max length<br>• The origin AS<br><br>all tied to the prefix owner's public key, and signed by the RIR's private key.
What is a ROA and who produces it?	A <b>Route Origin Authorization</b>.<br><br>• The IP prefix owner generates a key pair and sends a certificate request to their RIR<br>• The RIR validates identity and ownership<br>• The RIR stores the signed ROA in a public database
Describe the four stages of the RPKI architecture.	• <b>IP prefix owners</b> submit certificate requests to their RIR<br><br>• <b>Regional Internet Registries</b> validate and store ROAs in a database<br><br>• <b>Validator</b> software in each AS downloads the ROAs and verifies the signatures<br><br>• <b>Routers</b> receive a cache of verified prefix/origin pairs and check BGP updates against it
What does an RPKI validator do, and where does it run?	• Runs on a <b>server inside the AS</b>, not on the router itself<br><br>• Downloads the ROA database and verifies all the signatures<br><br>• Builds a local list of trusted prefixes and their origin ASes<br><br>• Installs that as a cache into the routers<br><br>Routinator is a common open source example.
What work does the router do under RPKI?	• <b>No cryptography.</b> All signature verification happened in the validator<br><br>• It holds a cache of verified prefix and origin pairs<br><br>• On each BGP update it checks the prefix state and accepts or rejects by policy and configuration
What are the three prefix states a router can find, and why does the third exist?	• <b>Valid</b> - prefix is in the database and the announced origin AS matches<br><br>• <b>Invalid</b> - prefix is in the database but the origin AS does not match<br><br>• <b>Unknown</b> - prefix is not in the database at all<br><br>Unknown exists for backwards compatibility. The Internet cannot upgrade every router at once, and after more than a decade adoption is still incomplete.
